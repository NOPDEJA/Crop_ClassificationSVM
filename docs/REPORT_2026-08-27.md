## 1. Summary

Four things happened since the last report.

1. **The corrected model improved twice, and the second improvement is the real one.** The
   parcel-disjoint run went from macro F1 0.2248 to 0.2283 after I repaired the protocol
   defects the last report listed, and then to **0.2344** after I added two spectral
   columns, raised the Stage-3 kernel capacity and applied a tuned operating point. That last figure is measured on
   5,500,269 test pixels that no model in the chain has ever seen.

2. **Adversarial review caught two errors of mine before this shipped, and one of them was
   in this report's first draft.** I claimed our operating-point sweep read 0.043 too high.
   It does not, it reads 0.0008 too high, and the 0.043 I quoted was a level difference
   between two parcel samples rather than a bias. Separately, I am withdrawing our standing
   claim that the rare crops are "not spectrally limited", because the probe it rests on
   splits by pixel and shares 87.5 % to 95.7 % of its rare-crop test parcels with training.
   I have since rebuilt that probe with parcel-disjoint halves, and it confirms the
   withdrawal: macro F1 falls from 0.5852 to 0.3945, the rare orchard crops lose 18 to 32
   points each, and Langsat goes from 0.68 to exactly zero. Half of what we were quoting was
   memorised ground.

3. **The cost-sensitive run I proposed does not work, and understanding why found the real
   defect.** Tempered class weights scored 0.2272 on the tuning half against 0.2294 without
   them, so the gate I declared beforehand stayed shut and no test-fold score was ever
   computed for that run. I have to be exact about what "unread" means here, because my
   first draft overstated it. Stage 1 still predicted fold 2 mechanically and the run log
   still printed the test fold's Stage-2 composition, so what the gate protected was the
   score, not the fold. But
   the weights came out at 1.00 almost everywhere, and chasing that down showed that
   **Stage 2 balances its four group labels while leaving the crop mix inside each group
   untouched**. Its plantation training pool is 96.8 % rubber and 0.08 % coconut. Replacing
   the learned Stage-2 route with the true group, every model frozen, is worth **+0.149**
   macro F1, which is about fifteen times the 0.0096 we have gained from the baseline to M5
   all year.

4. **The collaborator now has a composed cascade**, which our §6 last time said they did
   not. That claim was written from their repository at an earlier commit and it is out of
   date. Their chain also has one property worth naming, which is that the pixels they
   evaluate on are drawn from the same ground their models were fitted on.

The unflattering summary is that two of the four items are corrections to things I wrote or
proposed myself, and the model gained 0.0096 macro F1 in total.

## 3. The problem I am actually fighting

The class imbalance between rubber and Langsat is **6,685 to 1 across the whole labelled
tile**, which is 12,320,950 rubber pixels against 1,843 Langsat ones. That ratio is not the
same in every fold, because the split balances parcel counts rather than pixel counts. **In
the test fold it is 16,942 to 1**, 3,235,887 against 191, and in fold 1 it is worse still,
because Langsat has only 13 validation pixels in the whole fold. So macro F1, which is an
unweighted mean over thirteen classes, is dominated by classes I have almost no data for.

What last report established, and what this report keeps finding, is that the dial which
moves this number is not the architecture. It is how priors are handled at decision time and
which population the score is computed over. Three measurements from the same trained M5
cascade, differing only in those two things:

| what varies | macro F1 |
|---|---|
| plain argmax at inference | 0.2140 |
| operating point (0.3, 0.6), chosen on the calibration half (which also fitted the sigmoids), evaluated once on the tuning half | 0.2294 |
| their 200k-per-class population, 14 labels including `others` | 0.3078 |

The first two are the tuning half of fold 1 and the third is the test fold under the
collaborator's protocol, so they are not three readings of one quantity, but the size of
each effect is the point. Moving the decision rule is worth about 0.015 and changing the
evaluation population is worth about 0.03 to 0.04, and neither of those touched the model at
all. Nothing I have trained this year has moved macro F1 by 0.03.

That is why I keep predeclaring what counts as success before running anything. With a dial
this powerful sitting next to the metric, it is very easy to report a tuning artefact as a
result, and I have now caught myself doing exactly that once, in §4 below.

## 4. What I tried this week, and it did not work

### The idea

If the caps alone leave the rare crops starved, then a cost-sensitive SVM should help. This
is a standard remedy in remote sensing (Waske et al. 2009 for cost-sensitive SVM in land
cover, and the RUESVMs work of 2020 for the imbalanced-crop case), and the natural partner
to the post-hoc prior correction family (Saerens et al. 2002) that the operating point in
§2 comes from. So I changed exactly one thing against M5 and ran it overnight, from 02:29 to
05:48 on 2026-08-26, three hours nineteen minutes.

The scheme is tempered rather than full inverse frequency. Each class gets
w = sqrt(n_max / n), computed from the counts of the model actually being fitted, after the
caps have been applied. Full inverse frequency at 6,685:1 would give a handful of Langsat
pixels the same total weight as all of rubber, and the operating-point sweep had already
shown that pushing all the way to uniform costs more than it buys.

### The gate, written down before the run

I wrote the predeclaration into `runs/s2_2018_3date_parcel_weighted/PREDECLARATION.md`
before launching, and the run itself was launched with `SKIP_TEST=1`. The rule was that the
operating point would be selected on fold 1's calibration half over a 169-cell grid and
scored once on the tuning half, and only if that beat M5's number from the identical
procedure would the test fold be read.

I need to say precisely what `SKIP_TEST=1` does, because I described it as stronger than it
is. It stops Stage 2 and Stage 3 from predicting fold 2 and it stops the end-to-end scoring,
which is the part that matters, and that part did not happen. It does not stop Stage 1.
Stage 1 predicts every row in the tile in one pass, so `stage1_prob_test.npy` was written at
03:03 that morning, and `run.log:124` prints how the test fold's Stage-2 candidates break
down by group. So the honest statement is that no test-fold score exists for the weighted
run, not that the fold was untouchable. My predeclaration promised more than the script
enforced, and I should have checked what the flag actually gates before writing that
sentence.

### What happened

| | M5 | weighted | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| cell selected on the calibration half | (0.3, 0.6) | (0.3, 0.6) | same cell |
| **tuning-half macro F1** | **0.2294** | **0.2272** | **-0.0022** |
| crops scoring at least 0.01 | 10 of 13 | 11 of 13 | +1 |

The gate stayed shut, so **no test-fold score was computed for this run** and 0.2344 remains
the number. The one thing the weights bought is Longan, which went from 0.0029 to 0.0130 on the
tuning half, and the cost was spread thinly over Rambutan, Jackfruit and Mangosteen.

### Why it did so little, which I did not anticipate

When I read the weights the run logged, most of them were 1.00:

| model | weights |
|---|---|
| Stage 2, four groups | all four exactly 1.00 |
| Stage-3 field | all three exactly 1.00 |
| Stage-3 plantation | rubber 1.00, oil palm 1.00, coconut 2.90 |
| Stage-3 orchards | durian 1.00 up to Langsat 6.54 |

The reason is that the caps had already done the balancing. Stage 2 caps every group at
200,000 and all four groups have more rows than that, so after capping the four counts are
identical and a weight computed from post-cap counts is exactly 1. The same thing happens in
the field expert, where rice, cassava and pineapple all hit the 70,000 per-crop cap. So the
experiment I described as "class weights on Stage 2 and all three Stage-3 experts" was in
practice class weights on the orchards expert plus coconut, and everywhere else it was a
no-op by construction.

That is a real finding rather than a bug. My first reading of it was that the cap schedule
already is our class-weighting scheme, so future imbalance work should go at the caps and
not at `class_weight`. Adversarial review said that conclusion was too broad, and chasing it
down turned up the most useful thing in this report.

### What the caps actually balance, and what they leave alone

Stage 2 caps each of its four target groups to 200,000 rows, so the four **group labels** are
perfectly balanced. But it samples uniformly *inside* each group, so the **crop subtypes**
within a group are untouched. Counting the routed candidate pool that the cap draws from
(`stage2_pool_composition.csv`):

| Stage-2 group | composition of the pool it samples uniformly | expected rows in the 200,000 draw |
|---|---|---|
| plantation | rubber **96.82 %**, oil palm 3.10 %, coconut **0.08 %** | rubber 193,645 · oil palm 6,196 · **coconut 159** |
| orchards | durian **66.74 %**, mango 12.05 %, jackfruit 10.44 %, ... Langsat **0.60 %** | durian 133,471 · **Langsat 1,196** |
| field | cassava 45.17 %, pineapple 40.41 %, rice 14.43 % | cassava 90,331 · rice 28,853 |

So the Stage-2 boundary that decides whether a coconut pixel ever reaches the plantation
expert is fitted on a population containing about **159 coconut pixels against 193,645 rubber
pixels**. Balancing the group totals did nothing about that, because coconut and rubber carry
the same group label. The imbalance was never removed, it was moved one level down where we
were not counting it.

The consequence is measurable. On the tuning half, the share of each crop that Stage 2 sends
to the right group, given it already passed Stage 1 (`stage2_routing_accuracy.csv`):

| rubber | pineapple | oil palm | longan | mangosteen | durian | cassava | rice | jackfruit | rambutan | Langsat | coconut | mango |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 85.4 % | 77.1 % | 76.2 % | 69.0 % | 61.0 % | 59.0 % | 58.4 % | 47.2 % | 44.2 % | 37.8 % | 28.6 % | **13.7 %** | **11.0 %** |

And if I freeze every model and replace only the learned Stage-2 route with the true group,
tuning-half macro F1 goes from **0.2294 to 0.3785**. That single decision is worth **+0.1491**
(`oracle_routing.csv`), which is about fifteen times the 0.0096 the whole year of work bought
from the baseline to M5, and sixty times the 0.0022 the class weights cost.

So the corrected conclusion is narrower and more actionable than my first one. **The caps
balance the label being predicted and do nothing about the subtype distribution inside it,
and Stage-2 routing is where the cascade actually fails.** That is the next experiment, and
it does not need a full cascade run, because Stage 1 and Stage 3 can be held frozen.

### A second negative result

I also ran the tree-merge probe, which collapses orchards and plantation into one Stage-2
subclass so that the misroute that kills the orchard crops becomes harmless. The cascade
error analysis predicted large recall gains from it. On the tuning half it scores **0.2158**
against M5's **0.2283** at the same fixed operating point, so it is worse, and the
prediction was wrong. It was a reasonable bet from the routing table and it did not pay.

## 5. Analysis

### The operating-point procedure, and a correction I nearly shipped

`m2_operating_point.py` selected the operating point by maximising macro F1 on fold 1's
tuning half and then reported that same half, which is selecting on the number you report. I
rebuilt it as `sweep_operating_point.py`, which selects on the calibration half and scores
the selected cell once on the tuning half.

My first draft of this section said the old procedure read **+0.0426** high, taking the gap
between the calibration score 0.2720 and the tuning score 0.2294 at the selected cell. **That
was wrong and adversarial review caught it.** Those are two different populations, so the gap
is mostly a level difference between two disjoint parcel samples, not selection bias. The
proof is that at plain argmax, with no selection of any kind, the same two halves already
differ by **+0.0478**. A selection effect cannot be smaller than the no-selection gap and
still be the explanation.

Measured properly, selection optimism is the best tuning-half cell minus the tuning-half
score of the cell chosen on the calibration half:

| | M5 | weighted |
|---|---|---|
| best cell on the tuning half | 0.2302 | 0.2272 |
| tuning half at the cell chosen on the calibration half | 0.2294 | 0.2272 |
| **true selection optimism** | **+0.0008** | **+0.0000** |

So the old procedure was wrong in principle and almost harmless in practice, because the
169-cell surface is nearly flat near its top. The honest procedure is still the one to keep,
and both runs select the same cell (0.3, 0.6).

The genuinely useful number here is the one I mistook for a bias. **Two disjoint parcel halves
of the same fold differ by about 0.048 at identical settings.** That is the parcel-sampling
variance of this metric, and it is twenty times the -0.0022 that closed §4's gate. The gate
comparison is still valid, because both runs were scored on the same rows and the level
difference cancels, but nobody should read a fold-1 number from one half against a fold-1
number from another.

This does not touch the 0.2344 test figure, which was always measured on the untouched test
fold. It touches every fold-1 number we have quoted from the old script.

The answer to the question I actually asked is not the one I wanted. **MTCI is last in both
models**, and it is last by a wide margin at Stage 1. I added MTCI and raw B11 in M3 partly

dismiss is that MTCI is the only red-edge-derived column we have, so it has no correlated
substitute to hide behind. B11's number is the opposite case and is probably understated,
because SWIR_RATIO and SWIR_NIR are built from the same SWIR bands and can cover for it.

The other thing I did not expect is that the two soil and moisture indices, MSAVI and BSI, top
both models and beat NDVI by more than two to one. For separating perennial tree crops that
are green in all three dates, how bare the ground underneath looks carries more than how
green the canopy is, and that is a sensible thing for the data to be telling us.

Two dates of the same index carry each other, so a single-column permutation understates a
family and even these three-date sums are a lower bound. Read them as a ranking, not as a
budget.

### A claim of ours I am withdrawing

We have been saying that the seven near-zero crops are not at the spectral limit, on the
strength of an isolated probe where the same crops reach 0.44 to 0.68 under balanced priors
and five dates, with Langsat scoring highest of all. **I am withdrawing that claim.**

`probe_dry_season.py` splits by a random pixel permutation, not by parcel. Replaying its
split with parcel IDs (`probe_replay_overlap.csv`) shows how much train and test share:

| crop | train parcels | test parcels | shared | share of test also in train |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Langsat | 14 | 16 | 14 | **87.5 %** |
| Rambutan | 139 | 139 | 133 | **95.7 %** |
| Longan | 82 | 84 | 79 | **94.0 %** |
| Coconut | 111 | 111 | 104 | **93.7 %** |
| Rubber | 668 | 712 | 388 | 54.5 % |

The leak is worst for exactly the rare crops the claim was about, and mildest for rubber,
which is the one crop the claim did not need. So the probe shows that pixels from
already-seen parcels are separable, and it says nothing about an unseen parcel. That is the
same mistake as §2 of the last report, in a different file, and it was ours.

What survives is narrower and I will state only that: **under a uniform prior, five dates and
higher kernel capacity, rare-crop pixels drawn from parcels the model has already seen are
separable.** Whether the signal generalises to a new parcel is unmeasured, and the probe has
to be rebuilt with parcel-grouped splits before anyone quotes it again.

The related count was also wrong. Langsat has **10** training parcels, not the 27 we have
been repeating, and one of those ten holds 1,310 of its 1,639 pixels.

correct rather than over-cautious.

**So what do I now believe about the ceiling?** Something in between the two readings I
predeclared, and I will not round it to whichever one is more convenient.

- The claim **as we stated it is dead**. We said the rare crops reach 0.44 to 0.68 under
  balanced priors. On unseen parcels they reach 0.24 to 0.44, and Langsat reaches nothing at
  all. Roughly half the number we were quoting was memorised ground.
- The signal is **not** zero either, and that still matters for §8's item 1. Even at the
  reduced parcel-grouped level, the isolated probe scores Rambutan 0.34, Coconut 0.38 and
  Mango 0.44, while the full cascade delivers those same crops at 0.018, 0.009 and 0.066 end
  to end. A gap of that size between an isolated classifier and the composed chain is not a
  spectral ceiling, it is a routing loss, which is exactly what §4's +0.1491 measures from
  the other direction.
- **Langsat is the exception and should be treated as one.** Eight test parcels and 360
  training pixels from 8 parcels is not a measurement. Its zero is consistent with the
  spectral limit and equally consistent with having no data, and this probe cannot separate
  those.

One more thing fell out of the rebuild that I did not go looking for. **The dry-season window
stops paying once parcels are disjoint.** On the pixel split, adding March and April took the
macro from 0.5226 to 0.5852, a gain of +0.063, and that gain is what made the dry dates look
worth chasing. On the parcel split, `wet3` scores 0.3950 and `all5` scores 0.3945, so the two
extra dates are worth nothing at all. The apparent value of the dry season was the leak, and
that removes most of the case for re-running the pipeline on five dates.

## 6. The XGBoost study, corrected

Section 6 of the last report said the collaborator's repository contained no composed
cascade. **That is now out of date.** Reading the repository at commit `a9a7ca0`
(2026-08-25), `code/inference/inference.py` chains three models, and it does it in a way
that is close to ours in spirit.

- Rows with P(water) at or above 0.56 are removed, then rows with P(building) at or above
  0.56 are removed from what is left, and the flat 14-class crop XGBoost classifies the
  survivors.
- Non-crop truth is mapped to 9999, called `others`, and their macro is over 14 labels
  including it. `others` is a class their crop model was actually trained on, capped at
  200,000 like every other class.
- Their crop report is computed on the filter survivors only.

So we have converged on the same idea from two directions, which is that false positives on
non-crop ground have to count somewhere. They handle it with a fourteenth class and we
handle it with a Stage-2 sink plus mapping non-crop truth to 0. Neither of us was doing this
three weeks ago.

### Our numbers under their protocol

I rewrote `rescore_collaborator_protocol.py` against that commit. Their building filter has
no analogue in our cascade, because buildings live inside our Stage-1 `others` superclass
along with roads and bare ground, so I report a bracket instead of pretending otherwise.

| protocol | rows | macro F1, 14 labels | macro F1, 13 crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| ours, strict, whole test fold | 5,500,269 | 0.2743 | **0.2344** |
| theirs, water filter only | 1,626,372 | **0.3078** | 0.2673 |
| theirs, with P(others) standing in for the building filter | 1,152,302 | 0.3060 | 0.2757 |

These are **two proxy sensitivity scenarios on our buffered fold-2 population**, and I want
to be careful not to call them a bracket. The stand-in drops strictly more non-crop rows than
a real building filter would and the water-only variant drops strictly fewer, so it is
tempting to say the truth sits between them, but the table above does not support that. The
two scenarios order oppositely on the two macro columns, 0.2673 against 0.2757 on the 13
crops and 0.3078 against 0.3060 on the 14 labels, so they do not bound anything. **What our
number would be under their exact protocol is unknown**, and it stays unknown until we run
one agreed population through one agreed denominator. What the table does show is the size of
the effect: population and metric definition alone move macro F1 by roughly +0.03 to +0.04
here, with the model completely unchanged.

### The two gaps that remain

1. **The 200,000-per-class cap.** It leaves rubber at 200,000 against Langsat's 191 in our
   test fold, about 1,000 to 1, instead of the tile's 6,685 to 1. Rare-class precision is
   largely a function of prevalence, so this is not a small difference in how hard the task
   is.

2. **The survivor-biased crop denominator.** Their crop metrics are computed after two
   filters have already removed rows, so the crop model is never charged for water or
   buildings that the earlier stages let through.

There is also a third thing, which is not a gap between us but a property of their setup
worth naming. **Their evaluation pixels are drawn from the ground their models trained on.**
`extract_crops.py` samples up to 200,000 eroded pixels per class for training and
`train_crops.py` splits that file 60/20/20 by row, and then `extract_all.py` independently
re-samples up to 200,000 pixels per class from the same tile for inference. Nothing
separates the two draws spatially. For every class with fewer than 200,000 pixels, and that
is every crop except rubber, the training draw is essentially the whole class, so the
inference draw lands on pixels the model was fitted on. The overlap is concentrated in
exactly the rare classes a macro average rewards most. This is the same pixel-level leakage
we found in our own protocol on 2026-08-25 and fixed with the parcel split, so I raise it as
a shared problem rather than as a criticism.

**I am still not proposing a joint table.** Until one population and one denominator are
agreed, a side-by-side would compare measuring sticks. The rescore script above is ready to
serve as the shared basis whenever we want to agree one.

## 7. Limitations

1. **The test fold has now been scored three times**, for the baseline, M0 and M5, and the
   weighted run never got a test-fold score because the gate shut. That is weaker than
   saying the fold was untouched, and §4 sets out why. `SKIP_TEST=1` blocks Stage-2 and
   Stage-3 test prediction and the end-to-end scoring, and Stage 1 predicts the whole tile
   regardless, so a test-fold probability array and a printed test-fold composition exist
   for a run I described as not reading fold 2. No selection of mine used them. Every score
   costs a little of the fold's independence, and I am treating further ones as expensive.
2. **Hyperparameters are still frozen** at values chosen under the old leaky pixel-level
   search. They cannot bias the test score, being fixed constants, but they may be
   suboptimal for the natural-prior regime.
3. **Fold supports vary a great deal**, because the split balances parcel counts rather than
   pixel counts. Rubber has 7.25 M training and 3.24 M test pixels. The result is honest for
   this test fold and it is specific to this fold's composition.
4. **Langsat should not be quoted per class.** It has 1,639 training, 13 validation and 191
   test pixels, and it scores 0.0000 everywhere. There is not enough of it to support any
   claim, positive or negative.
5. **M0 to M5 is not attributable**, as stated in §2, because features and the operating
   point changed together.
6. **Run-to-run noise exists at full scale.** The weighted run and M5 routed 16,832,166 and
   16,832,158 pixels into Stage 2 respectively, an eight-pixel difference from identical
   code paths, which is threaded floating point rather than the class weights. It is far too
   small to explain the -0.0022, but it means I should not read the fourth decimal place of
   anything here.
7. **The isolated probe leaked parcels** and the claim that rare crops are not spectrally
   limited is withdrawn, as §5 sets out. The parcel-grouped rebuild is done and it does not
   restore the claim at the strength we stated it. What it leaves standing is narrower: the
   isolated gap between probe and cascade is a routing loss, not a spectral ceiling, for
   every rare crop except Langsat, where 8 test parcels cannot tell the two apart.
8. **Two disjoint parcel halves of fold 1 differ by about 0.048 at identical settings.** Any
   fold-1 figure is specific to its half, and only same-half comparisons mean anything.
9. Langsat's tuning-half support is **7 pixels from 2 parcels**. Its per-class number is
   noise and should never be quoted alone.
10. Labels are still the 2018 survey, and the mixed-crop compound codes are still excluded.

## 8. Next steps

In the order I would spend time on them.

1. **Fix the Stage-2 subtype mass.** Straight out of §4. Keep the 200,000 rows per group and
   the frozen Stage-1 and Stage-3 models, and change only how the loss is distributed across
   crop subtypes inside each group, normalised so each group's total mass stays fixed. Two
   paired Stage-2 fits, not two full cascades, so roughly two hours rather than seven. The
   oracle-routing gap of +0.149 says this is where the headroom is.
2. ~~Rebuild the isolated probe with parcel-grouped splits.~~ **Done, and reported in §5.**
   It cost ten minutes. The claim does not come back at the strength we stated it, the paper
   has to say we were half wrong about the ceiling, and the dry-season window turns out to
   be worth nothing once parcels are disjoint. What replaces this item is smaller and more
   specific: **get more Langsat and Longan parcels, or stop reporting them per class.** Both
   are at the point where the measurement, not the model, is the binding constraint.
3. **An honest hyperparameter retune** with `GroupKFold` on parcel ID and `scoring='f1_macro'`,
   on Stage 2 or the orchards expert alone rather than the whole cascade. The current values
   came from a pixel-level leaky search scored on **accuracy**, which is a larger validity
   defect than any choice of sampler. The literature warns that C, gamma and the class costs
   are not separable, so they have to be searched together.
4. **Difference-of-date features.** Rubber defoliates between October and December, which is
   the one signal in our existing acquisition window that we have never given the model
   directly (Cai et al. 2022, and Wang et al. 2023 in Nature for the regional picture). No
   new imagery is needed.
5. **Red-edge columns, with the §5 result attached.** We compute MTCI but we do not feed
   the raw red-edge bands, and the chlorophyll red-edge literature (Clevers and Gitelson
   2013) suggests they carry information MTCI compresses away. But MTCI came last in both
   permutation probes, so this is now a weaker idea than it was on Monday and I would put
   it behind items 1 and 2. If the raw bands do nothing either, red edge is finished for
   this tile and we should say so.
6. **Parcel-majority aggregation as a labelled side analysis**, not as a headline number
   (Belgiu and Csillik 2018, Matvienko et al. 2020). It changes the unit of analysis, so it
   must never be compared against a pixel-level figure, but it is what an end user of a land
   use map would actually do with the output.
7. **Permutation importance over the full cascade**, not just Stage 1 and the orchards
   expert.

For the paper's framing, Chen et al. 2024 is the reference point to cite for the ceiling.
They map crops at 10 m in eastern Thailand at 85.6 % overall accuracy, but with a dense
2017 to 2023 harmonic time series, so their number says what a long series buys and not what
three dates can reach. Foody 2020 is the reference for metric protocol, and Waśniewski et
al. 2022 with Turkoglu et al. 2021 for the hierarchical against flat question that §6 is
really about. Texture is cited but not built (Nomura and Mitchard 2018), and it stays out of
scope until the cap question is settled.

Deliberately not doing: ordinary pixel-level SMOTE, because with 1,639 Langsat pixels from
10 parcels about 98 % of a Langsat pixel's nearest neighbours belong to its own parcel, so
SMOTE would densify parcels we already have rather than add new ones. Also not equalising
every orchard crop down to Langsat's 1,639, which would change sample size, kernel capacity
and parcel coverage at once. Also not a second full-cascade retrain before item 1 is done,
no per-crop manual thresholds (limitation 9 makes them untunable honestly), and no joint
table before §6's two gaps are agreed.
